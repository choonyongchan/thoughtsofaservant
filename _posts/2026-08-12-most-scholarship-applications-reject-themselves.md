## Thank You, Next

"Thank you for your time, but with respect..."

Every rejection letter is a variation on that sentence. In 2019, Stanford Medicine turned the feeling into a [parody](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uqhao49IwB8) of Ariana Grande's "thank u, next", and anyone who has refreshed an application portal at midnight knows the tune without being taught it.

This year I ended up on the other side of the letter.

UCLA's Samueli School of Engineering invited me to assess scholarship applications. I still do not know why they picked me, but I said yes, and I spent several weeks with six of them, undergraduate and graduate, each read against a rubric.

Six is a small number and nothing here is a statistical finding. But these applications, read slowly, with real money attached to the outcome, were enough to show me something I did not expect.

I had assumed the job would be the one people describe: a stack of excellent candidates, and the slow agony of deciding which excellent candidate is fractionally more excellent. That is not what happened. Most of them never reached the stage where I compared anyone to anyone. They removed themselves before I got there.

That is a bleak sentence if you are the one applying. Sit with it for a moment and it becomes the most encouraging thing I can tell you. The top of the pile is far less crowded than it looks from the bottom.

I am writing this down for my juniors, the ones with an application cycle still ahead of them. The three tips below share a property: each is straightforward if you start early, and close to impossible in the fortnight after results day. That is the reason this is a post rather than a private observation.

## A Warning About Advice

Singapore's Public Service Commission receives [thousands of applications a year}(https://www.psd.gov.sg/newsroom/engineering-scholarships/) and awards a [few dozen](https://www.psc.gov.sg/resources/news?show=Speeches&for=All+Years&page=14&datapopup=keynote-address-by-prime-minister-lee-hsien-loong-at-the-2009-psc-scholarships-award-ceremony-on-saturday-25-july-2009-2.30pm-at-shangri-la-hotel) scholarships. A ratio like that produces an entire folk industry of speculation about what the board secretly wants.

The PSC has actually answered, [three times](https://www.psc.gov.sg/resources/chairman%27s-open-letters), in public. Then-Chairman Eddie Teo published an open letter titled "The PSC Interview" in July 2009, a year into the job, followed by a second letter in 2013. Current Chairman Lee Tzu Yang published his own in 2018, built around the line that scholarships are not rewards but opportunities for responsibility to serve. I was interviewed by Mr Lee, and I can tell you the letters are not decoration. They describe the thing they say they describe.

Read them. Read your seniors too, and take the coffee chat with the person who got in last year.

Then watch for the failure mode nobody warns you about. Advice about what a board wants to hear is very effective at producing an application the board wants to hear, and completely silent on whether that application is true to you. Use the advice to describe yourself more clearly, not to describe someone else more convincingly.

The stakes on that are higher than they look at 17 years old. Win a place by performing a person you are not, and you have won the wrong place. You then spend years (and in Singapore, often a bond) living inside a decision that a fictional version of you made on your behalf.

## The Easy Tip: Finish the Form

That is the entire tip. Finish the form.

I did not expect to write that sentence either. Two answers I graded carried a stated minimum of 300 words and ran THREE to FOUR lines. I re-read it to check I had not missed a scroll bar. I had not.

Underneath the near-blank answers sits a subtler category, and this is the one worth your attention: applications that are technically complete. Every compulsory field filled. Every optional field skipped. Nothing is missing. Nothing is offered, either.

![An application form split into two halves: the compulsory questions filled in, the optional questions left blank](/assets/images/posts/application-two-halves.svg)

If I were designing the form, I would put some of the most revealing questions in the optional section deliberately. Compulsory questions tell me what you will do to be considered. Optional questions tell me what you will do when nobody is checking. That is the closest thing a paper application has to a character reference, and it is free to give.

I do understand how the blanks happen. I have been the applicant with a row of scholarship application portals open across a row of tabs, and after a while the questions blur into one long question you have already answered. I just wanted to get them over and done with.

Look at the calendar and the blanks stop being mysterious. In Singapore, [PSC](https://www.psc.gov.sg/scholarships) and most statutory board portals open around August or September. The universities open theirs in October or November. A-Level results land in late February, and nearly everything closes by mid-March. That is a window of six months or more, and almost nobody uses more than the final three weeks of it, because the final three weeks are the part that feels urgent. Six applications in three weeks is arithmetic before it is anything else, and the optional questions are what the arithmetic eats first.

Nothing forces that timeline on you. You can open the form in September, when the only thing competing for your attention is the form. What fixed it for me was advice from my scholarship officer:

> Pick at most three scholarships or universities. Then give your best shot at all three.

Applying everywhere at partial effort feels like maximising your chances. It divides them. Effort is legible on an application: a reviewer can feel the difference between an answer written for this form and an answer recycled into it at midnight. There is no version of spreading yourself thin that survives contact with someone reading them back to back against a rubric and keen discernment.

Starting early is what makes that advice affordable. Three applications done properly takes months of thinking and a fortnight of writing. Attempt the same three in the fortnight alone and you will produce eight, badly.

## The Medium Tip: Tell Me About You, Not About the Topic

Most of what I read was generic. Coherent, organised, grammatical, but interchangeable. I could have swapped the names across most of those essays and noticed nothing.

Some of it was probably drafted wholesale with ChatGPT. Some of it was probably modelled on a "best scholarship essay answers" page found on Google Search. I have no objection to the tools, and I say that as someone who writes about them for fun. My objection is mechanical. A language model is built to produce the most probable answer to the question in front of it, and the most probable answer is, by construction, the answer most likely to arrive on my desk from somebody else as well.

So here is a test you can run on every sentence you submit:

> Could another applicant have sent me this exact sentence?

If the answer is yes, that sentence is not working for you. Cut it, or replace it with the version only you could have written.

Take two answers to the same question about wanting to teach.

The first explains what good pedagogy is, cites a philosophy of education, and argues that teachers shape society. All true. I had also read a close cousin of it earlier in the same batch.

The second says: I volunteered at a childcare centre because I like children and wanted to help them learn. I ran academic coaching. By my second year I was organising events with the other volunteers and the parents. I came out of it more resourceful than I went in, and I learned that patience is a logistical skill as much as an emotional one.

The second candidate wins, and it is not close. The story is ordinary, which is exactly why it works: it happened, I can picture it, and nobody else could have handed it in.

![Two answers to the same question side by side: a generic one that any applicant could have written, and a specific one that only its author could have written](/assets/images/posts/two-answers-compared.svg)

Your experiences are the only section of your application that is structurally impossible to plagiarise. Spend your word count there.

## The Hard Tip: Know Why This One, and Be Willing to Walk Away

Of the six, one or two did this well.

They could name the moment their interest in engineering became specific rather than general. They could list the projects, initiatives and internships that followed from it, and say what each one taught them. They could state what they intended to do next, and explain what this scholarship would let them do that they could not otherwise. Every part connected to the part before it. Reading one of those straight after the others is like a window opening.

This tip is hard because it requires you to have actually decided something.

It also carries a consequence that most application advice steps around. If you look honestly at who a scholarship is designed for and you are not that person, the correct move is to not apply.

![Two overlapping circles, what you want to build and what this scholarship funds, with the overlap marked as your case](/assets/images/posts/scholarship-fit-overlap.svg)

I mean that literally. Declining a mismatch costs you one line on a shortlist. Accepting a mismatch costs you years, sometimes a bond, served inside work you did not want. Scholarships exist to fund particular people into a particular future that the funder is trying to build (and a university major is the same bargain in a different wrapper). Where your future and theirs do not overlap, winning is the bad outcome.

Once you have decided a scholarship does fit, show the board the growth rather than the trophies. A reviewer looking at a coherent trajectory starts extrapolating almost involuntarily. They are working out what this person does with four more years and somebody else's money. Your job is to make that extrapolation easy and generous.

## Where These Tips Go Next

None of this is specific to scholarships.

In a job interview, "finish the form" means answering the whole question, including the half you find awkward, instead of the half you rehearsed on the train. In a university essay, "tell me about you" means recognising that the prompt is a request for evidence, not for your opinion of the field. And "why this one" appears in every interview you will ever sit, usually phrased as "why do you want to work here". The only good answer is the one where you have read what they actually do and can name the specific part you want to be inside.

Same three tests, different room.

## What I Took Away

My principal gave me one line during my own applications, and I have used it for decisions well outside scholarships since:

> Know what you want, what you don't want, and why you want it.

The first two are your filter. The third is your case. Most applicants can produce a case on demand and have never seriously done the filtering, which is why so many applications read as persuasive and land nowhere in particular.

None of this guarantees you anything. Fit is real, and in any given year there will be a candidate who matches a specific board better than you do. What these three tips buy you is entry into the group being compared at all, which, judging by what crossed my desk, is most of the battle.

If you want a second pair of eyes, go and find them. Seniors, teachers, and the friends who will tell you the true thing rather than the comfortable thing. When a friend says a paragraph feels vague, they are reporting precisely what a reviewer will feel and be far too busy to write down. This only works if you are early enough to do something about what they tell you; feedback received the night before a deadline is just bad news. ## Food for Thought

One thing I have not resolved.

Every reviewer, myself included, rewards the applicant who can narrate a clean line from an early interest to a present plan. Real lives are considerably messier. Some of the most capable people I know arrived at their work sideways, late, or by accident, and they would have scored badly on my rubric at 17 years old.

![Two routes from age twelve to age twenty-five arriving at the same point: a straight navy line, and a wandering red dashed one](/assets/images/posts/straight-line-vs-real-life.svg)

So which are we actually selecting for: the people with the clearest direction, or the people best at constructing one in hindsight? I do not think those are the same group, and I am not sure the rubric can tell them apart.

If your interests have not resolved into a straight line yet, that is a limitation of the instrument rather than a verdict on you. Write the truest version of your line anyway. Just go in knowing what you are being scored on.
